Replies stop it automatically

Connect your mailbox and the app reads it, matches each reply to the email it answers, and cancels the rest of that sequence on the spot. A positive reply moves the prospect to Interested; an opt-out suppresses the address and its whole domain, permanently.

It only ever reads — nothing in your mailbox is marked, moved or deleted. Nobody who said yes gets a "sorry I missed you" a week later.

The model

Seven things decide whether a machine earns its floor space

Each is scored 0–10 and weighted into a single number out of 100. The weights sum to exactly 1.0, which the test suite checks.

24%Traffic

How many people pass through daily, nudged by size clues in the map data — hotel rooms, apartment units, storeys.

22%Dwell

How long somebody is stuck there with nothing to do. A laundromat is 90 minutes; a hardware store is six.

16%Captivity

Measured, not assumed: every store, cafe, drive-thru and gas station within 400m is counted and docks the score.

10%Winnability

How locked-up the category already is. This is the one that matters most.

10%Hours

Whether the machine sells around the clock or only nine to five.

10%Access

How easy it is to service — parking, ground floor, and hours a driver can actually turn up.

8%Route density

How many other prospects sit within 1.5km. Windshield time is most of what a route costs to run.

Why winnability is the one that earns its keep. A hospital scores 10 for traffic and 10 for dwell — and a one-truck operator will essentially never win it, because a national contract already has it. The model knows that, so laundromats and machine shops rise above hospitals and schools. Anything else would hand you a ranked list of doors that don't open.

Run the real one

Three steps, then it's a double-click forever after

The working app runs on your own computer — your prospect list, your notes and your email account stay there. Nothing is uploaded anywhere.

Download the folder

Grab the repository from GitHub and unzip it wherever you keep your work.

github.com/mackmedley/Machine-Locator

Double-click the launcher

Machine Locator.command on a Mac, Machine Locator.bat on Windows. The first run sets itself up; after that it opens in seconds.

Press "Find prospects"

It scans your metro and ranks what it finds. To send outreach, add your business details and email account in Settings first.

Why this preview can't do the real work. Scanning OpenStreetMap and sending email both need a server, and a shared web page is the wrong place for your mail password and your prospect list. So the page you're reading is the interface; the app on your machine is the tool.

Want it on a URL of your own? There's a Docker image and a one-click Render blueprint in the repo. Because the database holds your mail password and a queue of email scheduled under your name, the app refuses to start on a public address until you set a password — then it puts a login in front of everything.

Sample data throughout — the businesses shown here are invented, and any resemblance to a real Oklahoma City company is coincidental. The working app finds real ones.
